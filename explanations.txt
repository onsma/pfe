RandomForest exposes model.feature_importances_ — a Gini/MDI importance: for every split in every tree, how much that split reduced impurity, summed and normalized across the whole forest. I:

1. Loaded the live artifact (preprocessor + model) exactly as the API does.
2. Pulled preprocessor.get_feature_names_out() (the transformed columns — one-hot dummies like nom__risk_class_Rejete, scaled numerics like winsor_num__tresorerie_disponible) paired with model.feature_importances_.
3. Aggregated one-hot dummies and derived-ratio columns back to their raw form-field names, and ranked within each category.

That's the ranking I used to redden the labels.

Why that's a different thing from SHAP
--------------------------------------

                | Global importance (what I used)                          | SHAP (what the app computes)
Scope           | One number per feature, averaged over the entire         | One value per feature, per individual client
                | training set                                             |
Sign            | Unsigned — magnitude only, no direction                  | Signed — tells you if this value pushed PD up or down
When computed   | Baked into the trained model, static until retrain       | Computed live at inference in shap_service.py, via shap.TreeExplainer
Question it     | "Which fields does the model lean on in general?"        | "For this client, what drove this prediction?"
answers         |                                                           |

So the red labels answer "which fields matter most across the model, in general" (a fixed, structural property of the trained forest) — that's the right question for deciding which fields deserve attention on a generic input form. The SHAP panel added separately (left of SHAP/LLM pages) answers "for the client currently loaded, what's driving their specific number" — a different, per-case question, recomputed every time you run Explain.

They usually agree directionally (payment_stress_index, tresorerie_disponible, chiffre_affaires_annuel are near the top by both measures) but won't always match exactly for any single client, since SHAP can surface a feature as locally decisive even if its global importance is modest.


====================================================================
Utility of Monte Carlo simulation in this platform
====================================================================

The Risk Prediction tab gives a single point estimate of default probability,
computed from the client's figures exactly as declared today. That number
hides a question every credit committee eventually asks: how much would this
PD move if the client's situation drifted — revenue softening, payment delays
creeping up, the macro backdrop turning? Monte Carlo answers it directly.

What it does, mechanically (monte_carlo_service.py)
----------------------------------------------------
1. Take the baseline client and a set of "shocks" — which raw fields to
   randomize and by how much (e.g. vary chiffre_affaires_annuel by +/-20%).
2. Draw n_simulations (e.g. 1,000-5,000) random multipliers per shocked
   field, normal- or uniform-distributed, and apply them to the baseline
   values. Financial magnitudes are clipped at 0 so they can't go negative.
3. Re-derive the engineered ratio features (loan_to_revenue, cash_to_debt,
   payment_stress_index, ...) from the shocked values, not reused from the
   baseline, so ratios stay internally consistent with each draw.
4. Run every simulated row through the SAME deployed pipeline
   (preprocessor.transform + model.predict_proba) in one vectorized batch.

The result is not one PD but a full empirical distribution of plausible PDs
for that specific client.

Why a single point estimate isn't enough
------------------------------------------
Two clients can share the same baseline PD yet carry very different risk:
one stays tightly clustered around its estimate under stress, the other
swings wildly. A point estimate can't tell them apart — only the spread of
the simulated distribution can.

What the analyst gets back (MonteCarloResponse)
--------------------------------------------------
- mean_pd / std_pd        — center and spread of the simulated distribution.
- percentiles (p5..p95)   — e.g. "5% of scenarios have PD below X".
- var_95                  — Value-at-Risk: the PD level breached in only the
                             worst 5% of simulated scenarios.
- expected_shortfall_95   — the AVERAGE PD within that worst 5% tail (more
                             informative than VaR alone, since VaR says
                             nothing about how bad the tail actually gets).
- prob_exceeds_threshold  — the share of simulations that would flip the
                             client to "default" under the decision
                             threshold — i.e. how close the client sits to a
                             downgrade even if its baseline PD looks safe.
- risk_distribution       — how many simulated scenarios land in
                             low/medium/high risk buckets.
- histogram + extremes    — full shape of the distribution, plus the exact
                             feature values behind the best- and worst-case
                             simulated scenarios.

Why this matters for credit analysis
----------------------------------------
- Dispersion, not just level: identifies fragile clients whose PD would
  swing a lot under realistic volatility, even if their current PD looks
  comfortable.
- Tail risk (VaR / Expected Shortfall): what provisioning and capital-
  adequacy discussions actually need — the worst-case severity, not the
  central estimate.
- Probability of a risk-class flip: quantifies how close a client sits to a
  downgrade, which a single PD number cannot show.
- Defensible stress testing: instead of one or two hand-picked
  "optimistic/pessimistic" scenarios, it explores the full space of
  plausible shocks at once — a more rigorous, auditable basis for
  stress-test documentation.

Practical tip: set wider stress bands on the fields already flagged as
high-impact (the red-labeled fields on Risk Prediction / the top SHAP
drivers) — shocking a low-importance field barely moves the distribution and
wastes simulation budget.


====================================================================
Monte Carlo — detailed mathematical explanation
====================================================================

Setup
-----
Let x in R^d be the baseline client's raw feature vector, and let

    f : R^d -> [0,1],      f(x) = P(default | x)

be the deployed pipeline (feature engineering -> preprocessor -> model). f is
deterministic — all randomness in the simulation comes from how the input is
perturbed, not from the model itself.

Step 1 — building a random input (code: monte_carlo_service.py:43-55)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Pick the shocked coordinates S (the "shocks" list). For each shocked field
j in S with baseline value b_j = x_j and shock size p_j ("shock.pct"), draw a
multiplicative factor Phi_j:

    Normal:    Phi_j ~ N(1, p_j^2)
    Uniform:   Phi_j ~ U(1 - p_j, 1 + p_j)

The shocked feature value is:

    X~_j = max(0, b_j * Phi_j)     if b_j >= 0   (financial magnitudes can't
                                                    go negative)
    X~_j = b_j * Phi_j              otherwise      (e.g. rates, which may)

Unshocked coordinates are held fixed: X~_j = x_j for j not in S. Together
this defines a random vector X~ in R^d — the distribution of a "wobbled"
version of the client.

Step 2 — Monte Carlo sampling
------------------------------
Draw n i.i.d. realizations X~^(1), ..., X~^(n) ("n_simulations", typically
1,000-5,000). For each, re-derive the engineered ratios (loan_to_revenue,
cash_to_debt, payment_stress_index, ...) so they stay consistent with the
shocked draw, then evaluate:

    Y_i = f(X~^(i)),    i = 1, ..., n

This is a Monte Carlo estimate of the pushforward distribution of f under
the perturbation — the law of Y = f(X~). There is no closed form for it
(tree ensemble + encoders + engineered ratios), so simulation is the only
practical way to characterize it. By the Law of Large Numbers, the empirical
distribution of {Y_i} converges to the true distribution of Y as n -> infinity.
By the Central Limit Theorem, the estimation error of any summary statistic
(e.g. the mean) shrinks as O(sigma / sqrt(n)) — independent of the input
dimension d, which is why a few thousand draws is enough even though the
client has dozens of features.

Step 3 — summary statistics (all computed from {Y_1, ..., Y_n})
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Mean and standard deviation:

    mu_hat    = (1/n) * sum_i Y_i                       -> mean_pd
    sigma_hat = sqrt( (1/n) * sum_i (Y_i - mu_hat)^2 )   -> std_pd

Percentiles — the q-th percentile is the order statistic at rank
ceil(q*n/100) of the sorted sample:

    p_q = Y_(ceil(q*n/100))   for q in {5, 10, 25, 50, 75, 90, 95}
                                                          -> percentiles dict

Value-at-Risk (95%) — the loss level exceeded by only 5% of scenarios, i.e.
the 95th percentile of the simulated PD distribution:

    VaR_alpha(Y) = inf { y : P(Y <= y) >= alpha },   alpha = 0.95
                                                          -> var_95

Expected Shortfall (95%) — the average PD WITHIN that worst 5% tail:

    ES_alpha(Y) = E[ Y | Y >= VaR_alpha(Y) ]
                ~= (1/|T|) * sum_{i in T} Y_i,   T = { i : Y_i >= VaR_alpha }
                                                          -> expected_shortfall_95

ES is a more informative tail measure than VaR alone: VaR only tells you the
threshold breached in the worst 5% of cases, not how bad those cases
actually get on average. ES is also, unlike VaR, a "coherent" risk measure
(subadditive) — it never understates the risk of a diversified portfolio.

Probability of breaching the decision threshold tau:

    P_hat(Y >= tau) = (1/n) * sum_i 1[Y_i >= tau]        -> prob_exceeds_threshold

— the empirical CDF complement evaluated at the decision threshold, i.e. the
fraction of simulated scenarios that would flip the client to "default".

Risk-class distribution: apply the same fixed cutoffs used elsewhere in the
app to every Y_i,

    risk_class(Y_i) = high    if Y_i >= 0.7
                       medium  if 0.4 <= Y_i < 0.7
                       low     if Y_i <  0.4

and count occurrences of each label -> risk_distribution.

Histogram: partition [0,1] into n_bins equal-width bins B_k and count how
many Y_i fall in each -> histogram (edges + counts).

Extremes: the k smallest and k largest Y_i (order statistics
Y_(1)..Y_(k) and Y_(n-k+1)..Y_(n)), reported together with the exact
shocked feature values X~^(i) that produced them -> extremes.

Why this is the right tool here
-----------------------------------
The model f has no closed form (RandomForest/XGBoost + encoders), so there is
no analytical way to propagate uncertainty in the input features through to
uncertainty in the predicted PD. Monte Carlo sidesteps that entirely: it
treats f as a black box and just evaluates it many times, which is exactly
why it is standard in quantitative finance for pricing and risk under
model uncertainty (Glasserman, "Monte Carlo Methods in Financial
Engineering", 2004) and why it is used here as the platform's stress-testing
engine.


====================================================================
How the most important features are known BEFORE running SHAP
====================================================================

This is possible because "most important feature" here means something
different from what SHAP measures — it is a property baked into the trained
model itself, not something computed per client at inference time.

Where it comes from
--------------------
A RandomForestClassifier is a collection of decision trees. Every internal
node in every tree is a single-feature split (e.g. "is tresorerie_disponible
<= 42,000?") chosen, during training, because it reduced impurity the most
among all candidate splits at that node. Impurity here is the Gini index — a
measure of how mixed the default/non-default labels are among the samples
that reach that node (0 = perfectly pure, max at a 50/50 mix).

For a given feature, its "importance" (Mean Decrease in Impurity, MDI) is:

    importance(feature j) = sum over all nodes that split on j of
                             ( samples_at_node / total_samples ) * impurity_decrease_at_node,
                             averaged over all trees in the forest, then
                             normalized so all feature importances sum to 1.

This is exactly the array returned by scikit-learn's
model.feature_importances_ — it already exists the moment the forest
finishes training. Loading it is just:

    artifact = load_artifact()          # loads the .joblib (preprocessor + model)
    importances = artifact["model"].feature_importances_

No inference on any specific client is required — this is why the ranking
was available before a single SHAP computation was run, and why the exact
same ranking is used to redden labels on the Risk Prediction form regardless
of which client is loaded.

Why this differs fundamentally from SHAP
------------------------------------------
MDI answers "across the ~10,000 rows the forest was trained on, which
features did it lean on most to separate defaulters from non-defaulters?" —
a single, static, global answer. SHAP (explained below) answers "for THIS
one client's numbers, which features pushed the prediction up or down, and by
how much?" — a different value for every client, computed on demand. The two
can (and often do) disagree for an individual case: a globally minor field
can be locally decisive if that particular client has an extreme value on it.


====================================================================
What SHAP actually does
====================================================================

SHAP stands for SHapley Additive exPlanations (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). It
answers one specific question for one specific prediction: "how much did
each input feature contribute to moving the model's output away from its
average/baseline prediction, for this exact row of data?"

Where the idea comes from (game theory)
------------------------------------------
The concept is borrowed from cooperative game theory — the Shapley value
(Shapley, 1953), originally designed to fairly split a payout among players
in a coalition based on their individual contribution. SHAP treats a
prediction the same way: the "players" are the feature values of one client,
and the "payout" to be fairly split is the gap between the model's average
prediction over the whole training set (the base_value) and its actual
prediction for this client.

Concretely, for a feature j, its Shapley value averages the marginal effect
of "adding" that feature's actual value across every possible order in which
features could be revealed to the model:

    phi_j = sum over all subsets S of features not containing j of
            [ |S|! * (d - |S| - 1)! / d! ] *
            [ f(S union {j}) - f(S) ]

where f(S) is the model's expected output when only the features in S are
known (the rest are marginalized out), and d is the total number of
features. In plain terms: look at every possible group of "other features
already known," measure how much adding feature j to that group moves the
prediction, and average that effect fairly over all possible groups and
orderings. This is the only attribution method that satisfies a set of
fairness axioms simultaneously (efficiency, symmetry, dummy, additivity) —
which is why it is preferred over simpler ad hoc attribution heuristics.

The key guarantee — additivity
----------------------------------
    base_value + sum_j(phi_j) = f(x)    for this specific client x

i.e. the SHAP values for one client always add up exactly to the gap between
the average prediction and this client's actual predicted PD. That is what
lets the SHAP page show "Base Value" and "Final PD" side by side with a
waterfall of per-feature contributions that reconciles the two exactly.

Why TreeExplainer specifically (shap_service.py)
----------------------------------------------------
The brute-force Shapley formula above is combinatorial — computing it exactly
would require evaluating the model on 2^d feature subsets, intractable for
~50 features. For tree-based models (RandomForest, XGBoost) there is an
efficient EXACT algorithm, Tree SHAP (Lundberg et al., 2018), which exploits
the tree structure to compute exact Shapley values in polynomial time by
tracking feature contributions as they flow down each tree's decision paths.
That is exactly what:

    explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(artifact["model"])
    shap_values = explainer.shap_values(transformed_client_row)

does — no approximation, no sampling, the mathematically exact Shapley
values for this model and this client.

What the app does with the output
--------------------------------------
1. shap_row = the array of phi_j values for every transformed column, for
   this one client.
2. top_idx = np.argsort(np.abs(shap_row))[::-1][:top_k] — take the top_k
   features by absolute contribution (magnitude, ignoring sign) so both
   strong risk-increasing and strong risk-decreasing features surface.
3. Each is tagged direction="increase_risk" if phi_j >= 0, else
   "decrease_risk" — this is what renders as the red/green bars and the
   Increases/Decreases risk labels on the SHAP page.
4. The same top_k list feeds the "highest-impact variables" panel on the
   SHAP and LLM pages, recomputed every time a new client (or what-if
   scenario) is explained.


====================================================================
What TEG, taux directeur, and PIB mean
====================================================================

TEG — Taux Effectif Global (field: taux_interet_teg)
--------------------------------------------------------
The "all-in" annual cost of the loan to the borrower, expressed as a single
percentage rate. It is not just the nominal/quoted interest rate — it also
folds in mandatory ancillary costs of getting the credit: application and
processing fees, mandatory insurance, guarantee/collateral registration
costs, etc. (per the dataset dictionary: "total effective rate proxy").
Two loans can have the same advertised interest rate but very different
TEGs if one carries heavier fees — TEG is what makes them comparable.
A higher TEG raises the client's real debt-servicing burden, which is why
it is one of the most influential fields in the Loan Details category of
the model (it interacts with cash flow and repayment capacity).

Taux directeur — policy / central bank rate (field: taux_directeur)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
The benchmark interest rate set by the central bank (in Tunisia's context,
the BCT's key policy rate) — the rate at which it lends to commercial banks.
It is a macro-economic input in this dataset, not something specific to one
client: it moves for the whole portfolio at once. It matters for credit risk
because commercial lending rates (including variable-rate loans, and
re-pricing at renewal for many SME loans) are anchored to it — a higher
policy rate raises borrowing costs economy-wide, tightens credit conditions,
and historically correlates with higher SME default rates, especially for
already-leveraged or thin-margin businesses. In this platform it is one of
the macro scenario fields (alongside taux_inflation, croissance_pib,
taux_chomage) used both as a direct model feature and to define the
economic backdrop of a client's file.

PIB — Produit Intérieur Brut / GDP growth (field: croissance_pib)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Gross Domestic Product growth — the year-over-year percentage change in the
total value of goods and services produced in the economy. It is the
headline measure of whether the economy is expanding or contracting.
For SME credit risk specifically, GDP growth is a leading indicator of
aggregate demand: when croissance_pib is low or negative (a slowdown or
recession), SMEs typically see revenue pressure, slower receivables, and
higher default rates across the portfolio, independent of any single
company's own fundamentals — this is exactly the kind of systemic risk that
the "Economic Scenario" (scenario_economique: normal / degrade / severe)
field and the Monte Carlo macro-shock parameters are meant to capture.


====================================================================
Choosing WHICH fields to stress in Monte Carlo: SHAP magnitude is a
proxy for sensitivity, not sensitivity itself
====================================================================

The Monte Carlo stress sliders are now populated dynamically from the top
SHAP-ranked fields of the currently loaded client (see app.js,
updateStressParamsFromShap), instead of a fixed list unrelated to the
client. This is a good practical default, but it is worth being precise
about what it actually optimizes for, because SHAP magnitude and stress
sensitivity are two different quantities that usually — but not always —
agree.

The distinction
------------------
SHAP answers an ATTRIBUTION question: "given this client's ACTUAL values
right now, how much did each feature contribute to moving the prediction
away from the average client?" It is a decomposition of the CURRENT
prediction, computed once, at the client's current point in feature space.

Monte Carlo needs a SENSITIVITY (local elasticity) question instead: "if
this feature's value moved by a plausible amount, how much would the
prediction change?" That is a question about the LOCAL SLOPE of the model
around the client's current point, not about the current attribution.

For a tree ensemble, these can diverge in either direction:
- A feature can have a LARGE SHAP value right now, yet sit in a region of
  the trees where nearby splits are far away — perturbing it further barely
  moves the prediction (its current contribution is large, but it is not
  "on a cliff edge").
- A feature can have a SMALL (near-zero) SHAP value right now — precisely
  because its current value sits close to a decision threshold, so it
  currently contributes almost nothing — yet a small stress could push it
  across that threshold and swing the prediction sharply. SHAP would not
  flag this feature as important for this client, even though it is exactly
  the kind of fragile point Monte Carlo exists to find.

Why the SHAP-based selection is used anyway
------------------------------------------------
1. It is free: the ranking is already computed by the time an analyst has
   run a SHAP explanation, so no extra backend round-trip is needed to
   populate the stress sliders.
2. In practice, for tree-based models, high current attribution and high
   local sensitivity correlate strongly: features that explain most of a
   client's deviation from baseline tend to be the ones sitting closest to
   the thresholds that matter for that type of client, so they are usually
   also where a stress shock has the biggest effect.
3. It keeps the tool's narrative coherent for a credit analyst: "we are
   stress-testing the variables you were just shown drive this client's
   risk" is intuitive and auditable, versus a separate, harder-to-explain
   sensitivity ranking.

The theoretically more rigorous alternative (not implemented)
--------------------------------------------------------------------
A direct local-sensitivity scan would answer the Monte Carlo question
exactly instead of by proxy: for each candidate raw field, perturb it by a
small fixed step (e.g. +/-1%) around the client's actual value, re-run the
model once per field, and rank fields by the resulting |delta PD|
(a finite-difference estimate of the local partial derivative
d(PD)/d(feature)). The top-K fields by this measured sensitivity would then
seed the Monte Carlo sliders, rather than the top-K by SHAP magnitude. This
costs one extra model evaluation per candidate field (~26 raw shockable
fields here — cheap for a RandomForest, since scikit-learn evaluates a full
forest on a batch of 26 rows in milliseconds) but requires an additional
backend round-trip before the stress panel can populate, which is why it
was scoped as an optional future refinement rather than built by default.


====================================================================
Worked example: reading an actual Monte Carlo result
====================================================================

A real simulation on the platform returned:

    P10 (best case)     48.3%
    P50 (median)         49.0%
    P90 (worst case)     50.2%
    Mean PD               49.1%
    Volatility (sigma)    0.7%
    VaR 95 (tail risk)   50.4%
    ES 95 (avg worst 5%) 50.7%

Why each of these is computed (recap, applied to these numbers)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- P10/P50/P90 bound the "typical" range: in 80% of the 2,000-5,000 simulated
  stress draws, this client's PD landed between 48.3% and 50.2%. The median
  (49.0%) is almost identical to the mean (49.1%), which itself is very
  close to the baseline PD — a first sign the distribution is not skewed by
  a few extreme scenarios.
- Volatility (sigma = 0.7 percentage points) is the standard deviation of
  simulated PD around the mean. This is the single most informative number
  here: it says that even under the configured stress bands (e.g. +/-20-30%
  swings on revenue, cash, payment delays...), this client's predicted PD
  barely moves. Compare this to a fragile client, where the same stress
  bands might produce a sigma of 8-10 points instead.
- VaR 95 (50.4%) is only ~1.3 points above the mean — the worst 5% of
  simulated scenarios are not much worse than the typical case.
- ES 95 (50.7%) — the average PD within that worst 5% tail — is barely
  above VaR 95 itself, meaning even the tail of the tail is not much worse
  than its own threshold. There is no "long tail" of catastrophic scenarios
  hiding beyond the 95th percentile.

What this particular result tells an analyst
----------------------------------------------------
A narrow spread like this (48.3%-50.7%, sigma under 1 point) means the
client's risk assessment is ROBUST to the stresses configured: whatever
happens to revenue, cash, or payment behaviour within the tested bands, the
PD stays anchored close to ~49%. That is a materially different, and more
useful, statement than "the PD is 49%" alone — it tells the analyst this
number can be trusted not to swing wildly under realistic adverse
conditions, which a single point estimate can never confirm by itself. (It
is also consistent with an earlier finding in this file: this particular
client profile sits in a region where the model's prediction is only weakly
sensitive to any single input, which is exactly why both its SHAP
attributions and its Monte Carlo volatility are small — see the "always
49%" investigation notes for the underlying cause.)


====================================================================
The decision threshold ("Seuil") on the Risk Prediction page: what it
is, and who sets it
====================================================================

What it is
------------
The model itself only ever outputs a continuous probability of default
(PD) between 0 and 1 — it never outputs "risky" or "not risky" on its own.
The threshold (tau) is the cutoff applied on top of that probability to
turn it into a binary decision:

    prediction = 1 (in default / high risk)   if PD >= tau
    prediction = 0 (not in default / safe)    if PD <  tau

This is exactly what backend/app/services/model_service.py does:
`pred = int(proba_default >= threshold)`. Changing tau does not change the
model's PD estimate at all — it only changes where the line is drawn on
that estimate.

Who chooses it, and why it is NOT a modeling decision
------------------------------------------------------------
The threshold is a business / risk-policy parameter, not something learned
by or derived from the model. It encodes the bank's risk appetite — how it
wants to trade off two kinds of mistakes:

- A LOW threshold flags more clients as risky: fewer real defaulters slip
  through undetected (fewer false negatives), but more genuinely safe
  clients get incorrectly flagged too (more false positives) — costing
  business by rejecting/over-scrutinising good clients.
- A HIGH threshold flags fewer clients as risky: fewer good clients get
  wrongly flagged (fewer false positives), but more actual defaulters slip
  through as "safe" (more false negatives) — costing money through missed
  defaults.

There is no universally "correct" threshold — the right value depends on
the bank's cost structure (cost of a missed default vs. cost of rejecting a
good client), regulatory capital requirements, and risk tolerance. That
decision belongs to the bank's credit risk policy / risk management
function, not to the data science team or the model.

Where it is actually set in this platform
------------------------------------------------
- Default value: the DEFAULT_THRESHOLD setting in backend/.env
  (backend/app/config.py), applied to every prediction unless overridden.
- Per-request override: POST /predict?threshold=0.4 lets an analyst test
  a different cutoff for a single call without changing the global default
  — useful for "what would the decision be under a stricter policy"
  exploration, without that exploration silently becoming the bank-wide
  policy.
